A typewriter simulated from the slug up

Nothing here is a recording. Each keystroke becomes a force on a simulated key, which flings a typebar at a rubber platen. The sound is what those contacts would make the machine's frame ring with. The letters on the page are printed by the same contacts.

1. Mechanics. Keys, linkages, 47 typebars, the escapement, mainspring, carriage, line-space ratchet, shift segment and bell run as rigid bodies in an audio worklet, eight steps per sample: 384,000 a second. A steel dog meeting a rack tooth is over in a few dozen microseconds.

2. Contact. When a slug reaches the platen, a Hunt–Crossley contact decides how far it sinks into the rubber, how long it stays (about 130 µs) and how hard it rebounds. The small trace in the corner is that force.

3. Sound. Every contact (slug on platen, typebar landing on felt, dog catching the rack, the ratchet clicking as the carriage flies home, the hammer on the bell) excites a modal model of the frame, the typebar and the bell. The strike point travels with the carriage, so every column rings the frame a little differently. Two pickups on the frame give you stereo.

4. Ink. The contact patch grows with the square root of force, so a light touch inks only the middle of each letter. Ink passes through a woven ribbon into paper fibres, and overstrikes add optical density. Each typebar is slightly bent and slightly off-square. Strike before the shift segment settles and you print half of two letters.

  • Type faster than the carriage can travel and your letters crowd together.
  • Hit two keys at once and the typebars jam at the platen. Esc pulls them apart.
  • The bell rings seven characters before the right margin. Enter throws the carriage home.

Drag to look around, scroll to zoom. In Controls you can change the machine, the platen's hardness, the ribbon, how worn the type is, or let a simulated typist take over.

Fitted rather than derived: the office machine's frame modes, matched to a recording of a 1930s Underwood. The portable and plastic frames and the weave of the ribbon are set by hand. Texts are in the public domain. Typeface: Courier Prime (SIL Open Font License).
